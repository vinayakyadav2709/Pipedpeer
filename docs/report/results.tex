% ============================================================
\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}
% ============================================================

The evaluation asks six questions. Does content-addressed caching make repeated
submissions cheap (Section~\ref{sec:res-cache})? What does interception cost
when it decides not to distribute (Section~\ref{sec:res-shim})? Does parallel
work cross machines, and is it shared well across unequal ones
(Section~\ref{sec:res-dist})? Does training across machines stay accurate, and
when does it pay (Section~\ref{sec:res-train})? Does a run survive the loss of a
machine (Section~\ref{sec:res-ft})? And what happens between real machines on
different networks (Section~\ref{sec:res-recorded})?

\section{Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:res-setup}

\textbf{Hosts.} Laboratory measurements ran on two development hosts: an Intel
i5-13420H with 12 hardware threads and 15\,GiB of memory under Fedora~43, and a
16-thread laptop with an RTX~3060 laptop GPU under Fedora~44.

\textbf{Container laboratories.} Worker daemons run in containers on one host.
In the \emph{plain laboratory} (three workers) the containers share the host's
processor, memory and loopback interface; it measures correctness, overhead,
cache behaviour and fault handling, and cannot measure multi-machine speedup.
Where a figure depends on speed or on the network, each container is given a
fixed processor and memory limit and the links between containers are shaped by
\texttt{tc netem} to the latency, bandwidth and loss of a LAN, a Wi-Fi network
or a 5G link, with up to 5\% loss; speedup is then one container against $N$
containers of the same size. A \emph{NAT laboratory} places each node on its own
private network behind a router container --- two cone and two symmetric
translators --- with the introducer on a public network. A
\emph{laboratory-layout} lab puts containers on one bridge network as a college
LAN, with the host's own daemon as the GPU machine, one container on multi-user
Nix, and a two-container lab sharing the host's GPU for training rings.

\textbf{Training accuracy.} The accuracy of each synchronisation setting is
measured on three models --- a four-layer perceptron sized for a GPU (MLP-G), the
same perceptron at a CPU sizing (MLP-C), and a small transformer --- with three
seeds per cell, against one device trained on the same data. Ring sizes beyond
two are measured in-process, with the ranks trained side by side on one machine.

\textbf{Real machines.} Two laptops in different homes (RTX~3060 and RTX~3050)
connected over a direct Tailscale path \cite{anderson2020nat}, a Samsung
Galaxy S20~FE phone under Termux, and further measurements between a 20-core
machine, a 16-core peer and a public cloud host acting as introducer.

\textbf{Workloads.} Seven example workloads cover a standard-library parallel
map, a map over a lambda, scikit-learn with \texttt{joblib}, NumPy linear algebra,
out-of-core pandas, torch data-parallel training and file synchronisation; one
script combines every kind of intercepted work, and five scripts written the way
a student would write them stand in for code the system has never seen. Raw
output for every measurement is kept in the project's \texttt{docs/benchmarks}
directory. Repetition counts are given with each table.

50\,MiB, the two caches on one node shrank from 109\,MiB to 28\,MiB and from
768\,MiB to 4\,KiB, and the next job still ran on its peer.

\section{Distribution Across Machines}
\label{sec:res-dist}

\textbf{Work crosses nodes.} With distribution forced, every one of five runs of
a 48-item parallel map on the plain laboratory reported in its receipt 18 items
completed on other daemons and the rest locally, with an identical checksum each
time. The NumPy workload moved its 34\,MB singular value decomposition to one
worker. On one host distribution can only add overhead, and the project's pool
benchmark shows how much: plain Python took 0.17\,s, a hand-tuned pool 0.17\,s,
and the untouched script under PipedPeer 0.24\,s with 12 of 48 items on the
cluster, within the $3\times$ budget.

\textbf{Sharing by measured speed.} On a laboratory cluster limited to 8, 2 and
1 CPUs, dividing a map by headcount ran 1.6 times slower than not distributing at
all. Dividing by measured throughput declined the one-CPU worker and took a
median of 2.87\,s, against 3.61\,s by headcount. With one peer held to a tenth of
a processor in a cluster of otherwise equal peers, four consecutive runs took
1.60, 1.25, 1.09 and 1.08 times the equal cluster's time, as the fit learned the
slow peer.

\textbf{Scaling.} A scaling map sweeps one variable at a time with the rest
fixed --- payload from 1\,KB to 64\,MB, 8 to 2048 items, per-item cost from
$10^3$ to $3\times10^6$ operations, two to four nodes, and a speed spread of up to
ten times --- and fits each step's growth on a log-log scale from the run
records, treating a step whose exponent exceeds the expected one by more than
0.15 as a defect. No step grew faster than expected, and four equal nodes ran a
map 2.9 times faster than one (0.81\,s against 2.37\,s).

\section{Training}
\label{sec:res-train}

\textbf{One GPU or a split.} The decision between a ring and one GPU matters in
both directions. In the laboratory, a small model with heavy steps on a LAN
trained in 9.1\,s split across two ranks against 11.8\,s on one GPU, while a
9\,MB model with light steps on Wi-Fi took 80.7\,s split against 8.0\,s on one
GPU. On one machine, the same model and batch took 1.5\,s on its GPU and 55.0\,s
on its CPUs.

\textbf{Fast synchronisation.} Table~\ref{tab:fastsync} and
Figure~\ref{fig:ressync} compare a two-rank ring under exact and fast
synchronisation with one device. Every model stays within the 1\% bar on both
link profiles, each gap within the spread of exact synchronisation's own gap,
while each rank sends nine to fifteen times fewer bytes. Fast synchronisation was
faster than exact on every model and profile, by 1.2--1.4 times on the LAN and
1.6--3.1 times on Wi-Fi.

\textbf{The outer step and the ring size.} DiLoCo's outer step (Nesterov momentum
0.9 at an outer learning rate of 0.7) failed the bar at every interval tried, the
transformer by $+21.7\%$ even at one synchronisation per epoch (156 local
steps). In in-process runs on the same three models, the largest interval that
passed on every model was 32 steps at two ranks, 16 at three and 4 at four; from
five ranks no interval passed, since at eight ranks the transformer failed at
every interval down to 2. Of the remedies the literature offers, only exact
synchronisation with rank-16 PowerSGD \cite{vogels2019powersgd} compression and
error feedback kept every model within 1\% at 4, 8 and 11 ranks (worst mean
$+0.18\%$), at $3.2\times$ fewer bytes than 8-bit exact synchronisation on the
perceptrons; the others (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}) failed. Fast synchronisation
therefore serves the two to four machines of a small group.

\textbf{Adapting the ring.} On a ring of 16, 8 and 2 cores, admission took the 16
and the 8, shared the work 66:34, and ran in 23.6\,s against 25.4\,s with equal
batches. Throttling one rank from 8 CPUs to 1 and back moved the shares from
64:36 to 84:16 and then to 70:30.

The upper block lists the criteria on which PipedPeer was designed to differ, so
it wins them by construction; the lower block is the more informative half
(Section~\ref{sec:security}). Spark, Ray and Dask are far more capable at scale,
with mature schedulers and tooling; PipedPeer trades maturity and scale for the
elimination of set-up, code change and network configuration. BOINC
\cite{anderson2004boinc}, closest in spirit, is a platform for specific projects
with their own servers rather than a general executor.
