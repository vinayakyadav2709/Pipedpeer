% ============================================================
\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}
% ============================================================

The evaluation asks six questions. Does content-addressed caching make repeated
submissions cheap (Section~\ref{sec:res-cache})? What does interception cost
when it decides not to distribute (Section~\ref{sec:res-shim})? Does parallel
work cross machines, and is it shared well across unequal ones
(Section~\ref{sec:res-dist})? Does training across machines stay accurate, and
when does it pay (Section~\ref{sec:res-train})? Does a run survive the loss of a
machine (Section~\ref{sec:res-ft})? And what happens between real machines on
different networks (Section~\ref{sec:res-recorded})?

\section{Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:res-setup}

\textbf{Hosts.} Laboratory measurements ran on two development hosts: an Intel
i5-13420H with 12 hardware threads and 15\,GiB of memory under Fedora~43, and a
16-thread laptop with an RTX~3060 laptop GPU under Fedora~44.

\textbf{Container labs.} Worker daemons run in containers on one host.
In the \emph{plain lab} (three workers) the containers share the host's
processor, memory and loopback interface; it measures correctness, overhead,
cache behaviour and fault handling, and cannot measure multi-machine speedup.
Where a figure depends on speed or on the network, each container is given a
fixed processor and memory limit and the links between containers are shaped by
\texttt{tc netem} to the latency, bandwidth and loss of a LAN, a Wi-Fi network
or a 5G link, with up to 5\% loss; speedup is then one container against $N$
containers of the same size. The \emph{LAN lab} reproduces a college
laboratory: containers on one bridge network, the host's own daemon as the GPU
machine and one container on multi-user Nix, with a two-container variant
sharing the host's GPU for training rings.

\textbf{Two-site testbed.} Joining across networks without Tailscale is
evaluated on a two-site container testbed on the same host: two containers on
separate private networks, both numbered 192.168.0.0/24, each behind a router
container whose iptables rules translate addresses and drop unsolicited inbound
packets, with the introducer on a third network standing for the internet. Every
user command is run across it.

\textbf{Training accuracy.} The accuracy of each synchronisation setting is
measured on three models --- a four-layer perceptron sized for a GPU (MLP-G), the
same perceptron at a CPU sizing (MLP-C), and a small transformer --- against
one device trained on the same data and seeds: two-rank rings in
the lab, larger rings in-process, with the ranks side by side on a single machine.

\textbf{Real machines.} Two laptops in different homes (RTX~3060 and RTX~3050)
connected over a direct Tailscale path \cite{anderson2020nat}, a Samsung
Galaxy S20~FE phone under Termux, and further measurements between a 20-core
machine and a 16-core peer joined over Tailscale, with a public cloud host
acting as introducer.

\textbf{Workloads.} Seven example workloads cover a standard-library parallel
map, a map over a lambda, scikit-learn with \texttt{joblib}, NumPy linear algebra,
out-of-core pandas, torch data-parallel training and file synchronisation; one
script combines every kind of intercepted work, and five idiomatic, unoptimised
user scripts stand in for unseen workloads. Lab
wall times are medians of three to five runs, except training rings, which are
one seed. Lab accuracy is the mean of three seeds; in-process accuracy is the
mean of seeds 0--2 and, where run, of seeds 0--5, and both must pass. Sizes in
KiB, MiB and GiB are binary ($2^{10}$, $2^{20}$ and $2^{30}$ bytes); KB, MB and
GB are decimal.

\section{Functional Verification}
\label{sec:res-tests}

The test suite exercises the properties the design relies on, with every
optional Python library present --- among them that a hash-shuffled aggregation
equals local pandas, that a parallel map whose every remote part fails still
returns the correct result, that the daemon's average is weighted by samples,
and that a link hello replayed on another session is refused. % source: docs/benchmarks/raw/T3_audit_tests.txt (95 caught, 0 missed).
A mutation audit
guards against a suite that passes without proving anything: it breaks each of
95 features in turn, from the sample-weighted gradient fold to the
router-mapping checks, and requires a test to fail. Every mutation was caught.

\section{Environment Cache}
\label{sec:res-cache}

The same trivial job was submitted five times to the plain lab
(Figure~\ref{fig:rescache}). The first submission, which also built the
environment, took 23.4\,s; the second landed on a worker that did not yet hold
the closure and took 8.1\,s; once the closure was in place, a submission sent
only the workspace and took a median of 0.83\,s.

    sent nothing. Right: every run, coloured by what its log says was sent.}

Against a container peer on the submitting laptop, the path-level difference
sent 29\,MB (7 of 43 store paths) instead of 206\,MB when one package was added
to an environment the peer held, and a peer holding an environment fetched
0.43\,MiB of a 444\,MiB closure for one that differed from it by one small
pure-Python package. In the LAN lab, a dry-run build of an unseen script's
environment took 4\,s on a container that had built the common packages ahead
of time, against 54\,s on one that had not
(\texttt{docs/benchmarks/rehearsal.md}).

With one of two nodes held to 800\,kbit/s, the first run waited the upload's
60\,s limit for the slow node and then started; the send to that node
continued in the background and held up no run, and once it had finished a
run took 2.1\,s with the slow node taking a share of the items. With the
cache cap lowered, both caches were pruned and the next job still ran on its

\section{Interception and Sandbox Overheads}
\label{sec:res-shim}

A 16-item parallel map whose tail is 0.01\,s of work against a 0.5\,s dispatch
floor is declined by the cost model; a forced run of the same workload does
dispatch, so the decline is the model's choice and not a dead cluster.
Declining costs about 0.12\,s per job, the measurement of the head of the map
plus the decision: a median of 0.80\,s against 0.68\,s with interception
disabled. Interpreter startup for a job that imports nothing heavy, best of five
runs, is $1.24\times$ that of plain Python when NumPy, pandas, torch and joblib are
patched on first import, and $20.0\times$ when they were patched at startup,
measured on another host (Figure~\ref{fig:resshim}). A continuous-integration check fails any
change that raises this ratio above $3\times$.

\section{Distribution Across Machines}
\label{sec:res-dist}

\textbf{Work crosses nodes.} With distribution forced, the node running a
48-item parallel map sent 18 items to three other daemons of the plain lab, one
of them the submitting machine's own, with results identical to a local run. The NumPy workload moved its 34\,MB singular value decomposition to one
worker. Because the plain lab's containers share the host's processor,
distribution there can only add overhead, and the pool benchmark shows how
much: plain Python took 0.17\,s, a hand-tuned pool 0.17\,s, and the untouched
script under PipedPeer 0.24\,s with a quarter of the items on the cluster.

\textbf{Sharing by measured speed.} On a lab cluster limited to 8, 2 and
1 CPUs, dividing a map by headcount took 3.6\,s (median of five), 1.6 times slower
than not distributing at all. Dividing by measured throughput declined the
one-CPU worker and took 2.9\,s, faster than the headcount split. Derived from
those two figures, it was still about 1.3 times slower than a local run, so at
this size the map is better kept on one machine.
With one peer held to a tenth of a processor in a cluster of otherwise equal
peers, the slowdown against an equal cluster fell from 1.6 times on the first
run to 1.08 by the fourth, as the fit learned the slow peer
(Table~\ref{tab:later}).

\textbf{Scaling.} With each container held to a fixed quota of two processors,
a scaling map sweeps one variable at a time with the rest
fixed --- payload from 1\,KB to 64\,MB, 8 to 2048 items, per-item cost from
$10^3$ to $3\times10^6$ operations, two to four nodes, and a speed spread of up to
ten times --- and fits each step's growth on a log-log scale from the run
records. No CPU step grew faster than its expected order. Four equal nodes ran a
map 2.9 times faster than one (0.81\,s against 2.37\,s): quotas that together
stay within the host's processor let this map measure how the cost grows with
the node count, which the shared plain lab cannot; it is not a speedup between
physical machines.

\section{Training}
\label{sec:res-train}

\textbf{One machine or a split.} The decision between a ring and one device
matters in both directions. In the lab, a small model with heavy steps on a LAN
trained in 9.1\,s split across two containers of two processors against
11.8\,s on one such container, while a 9\,MB model with light steps on Wi-Fi
took 80.7\,s split against 8.0\,s on one container. A GPU widens the gap: the
training job of Table~\ref{tab:recorded}, measured in one run on one machine,
took 1.5\,s on an RTX~3060 against 55.0\,s on its processors.

\textbf{Fast synchronisation.} At the default interval for two ranks, 32
steps, in-process runs kept every model within the 1\% bar: MLP-G $+0.06\%$,
MLP-C $+0.64\%$ and the transformer $+0.73\%$ over seeds 0--2, and MLP-C
$+0.35\%$ and the transformer $-1.02\%$ over seeds 0--5. Table~\ref{tab:fastsync}
gives one lab run of a two-rank ring at that interval: each rank sent 7.6 to
9.2 times fewer bytes than under exact synchronisation, and the ring finished
1.2--1.4 times faster than exact on the LAN and 1.6--3.1 times on Wi-Fi. A
separate lab run at 64 steps, three seeds per cell
(Figure~\ref{fig:ressync}, right), also stayed within the bar on both
profiles: MLP-G $+0.64\%$/$+0.61\%$, MLP-C $-2.14\%$/$-2.09\%$ and the
transformer $+0.18\%$/$+0.50\%$ (LAN/Wi-Fi), each within the spread of exact
synchronisation's own gap; MLP-C's negative gap reflects that periodic
averaging can generalise slightly better than exact synchronisation
\cite{lin2020localsgd}. The default is 32 rather than 64 because in-process the
transformer failed at 64 on seeds 0--2 ($+2.13\%$).

\textbf{The outer step and the ring size.} DiLoCo's outer step (Nesterov momentum
0.9 at an outer learning rate of 0.7) failed the bar at every interval tried,
leaving the transformer $21.7\%$ above one device even with one synchronisation
per epoch (156 local steps), the longest interval these runs allow. In-process
runs on the same three models, on up to six seeds, set the interval $H(n)$ as
the largest that passed on every model: 32 steps at two ranks, 16 at three and
4 at four; at eight ranks the transformer failed at
every interval down to~2, so no interval is used for five or more ranks. Of ten methods tested at 4, 8 and 11 ranks, only exact synchronisation
with rank-16 PowerSGD \cite{vogels2019powersgd} compression and error feedback
kept every model within 1\% (worst mean $+0.18\%$), at $3.2\times$ fewer bytes
than 8-bit exact synchronisation on the perceptrons but $1.7\times$ more on the
transformer (Table~\ref{tab:later}). Fast synchronisation therefore serves the two to
four machines of a small group.

\textbf{Adapting the ring.} Offered ranks of 16, 8 and 2 CPUs, containers
with those CPU quotas sharing one host, admission took the 16 and the 8, shared
the work 66:34, and ran in 23.6\,s against 25.4\,s with equal batches.
Throttling one rank's container from 8 CPUs to 1 and back moved the shares from
64:36 to 84:16, as the step-time window saw the throttled rank slow down, and
then to 70:30 after it recovered.

\textbf{Across two NATs.} On the two-site testbed of
Section~\ref{sec:res-setup}, scripts, parallel maps, remote runs and both
training sync modes ran across the sites, and a node whose link was cut mid-run
was replaced. Two machines meet directly when one introducer is reachable;
otherwise a public address or an overlay such as Tailscale is required. The
two laptops above ran over Tailscale for that reason: neither home offered a
reachable introducer (one router refuses port mapping, the other machine sits
behind two NATs). No relay is involved in either case.

\textbf{A phone.} On a Samsung Galaxy S20~FE (Android~13, Termux), a laptop's
CPU script spread over that laptop, a second laptop over the internet and the
phone gave the phone 8 of 192 parallel items with an unchanged result, after the
phone had built the job's environment from the binary cache in 27\,s. A torch
script training 160 small models gave the phone 6 of them, all converged;
with 32, it took none.

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} sets PipedPeer against Spark, Ray, Dask, BOINC and
plain \texttt{ssh}. The upper block lists the criteria on which PipedPeer was designed to differ, so
it meets them by design; the lower block is the more informative half

elimination of setup, code change and network configuration. BOINC
\cite{anderson2004boinc}, closest in spirit, is a platform for specific projects
with their own servers rather than a general executor. No benchmark was run
against these systems; their entries come from their published designs.
